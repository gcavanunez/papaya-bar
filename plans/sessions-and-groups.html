Two things become one

Today Papaya has two features that save the same thing (tabs, groups, windows) in two places that never meet. That's why "Save all as session" and "Save current tabs as context" feel like the same button with different consequences.

Today · saved sessions

A copy

  • Listed on the Sessions page
  • Never changes after you save it
  • "Open" adds its tabs next to what's open
  • No history

Stored in chrome.storage

Today · contexts

A workspace

  • Only in the side panel and the palettes
  • The active one keeps up with your open tabs
  • Switching saves what's open, closes it, reopens another
  • Last 10 versions, but no screen shows them

Stored in the TinyBase database

→

After · one library

Sessions

  • Every session in one list: the Sessions page, the sidebar and ⌘K
  • One open at a time; you start in Home
  • Switch to any of them, old saved sessions included
  • Versions for every session, with a screen to see and restore them
  • Rename, notes, archive, download; edit the tabs of a closed one (proposed)

One store, its own; the old one stays as the rollback copy

One change to the phase split

Phase 2 was going to end with today's screens running on the new data. Your data rules that out: the contexts code rebuilds every context from every saved tab on each refresh, and the side panel refreshes on every tab change, so 162 sessions holding 15,048 tabs would make it crawl. So the old screens keep the old store until the new library screen replaces them, and the switch-over happens then, as the first step of phase 4. Until that moment nothing about your stored data changes.

1What changed since v1

  • Groups are now the everyday unit. You stash a group to close its tabs and keep it listed in the sidebar, and you open it again with one click. v1 could only "split out" a group into a separate session, which is now Move to session.
  • Switching between the groups you have open is a core feature, separate from switching whole sessions.
  • Smart grouping moves to phase 1 and doesn't wait for the backend. Chrome's own on-device model can group tabs, with replies checked against a schema.
  • Your answers from every review are recorded as decisions; none are open. Your follow-up questions (loading big sessions, the Chrome API behind it, pinned tabs) are answered below, and the keys for switching are spelled out.

2The model

One session is open at a time; it's everything in your windows. Inside it, tabs are either in a group or loose. A group is open (a Chrome tab group you can see) or stashed (closed, waiting in the sidebar). Pinned tabs belong to no session. Closed sessions and their history make up the past.

Illustration with example names. The orange boundary is one session; only one is open at a time.

Definitions
Thing What it is Replaces
Session Your workspace: groups, loose tabs and window layout. One open at a time. You start in Home. Contexts, saved sessions, the Inbox
Group Related tabs with a name and color. Open: a Chrome tab group. Stashed: closed, listed in the sidebar. Chrome tab groups, plus "peel off to context"
Loose tabs Open tabs that aren't in a group yet. The Inbox
History A version is saved when you close or switch away from a session, or click "Save version". The last 10 are kept. Context snapshots
Parked A stashed group that stale tabs move into automatically. The Parked context

Where you switch from, and the keys

Shift isn't a shortcut on its own. It changes whichever way you switch from focus to swap (assuming focus is the default):

From Focus Swap
Sidebar Click the group Shift-click
Palette ⌘K on Papaya's pages, ⌥K on any website: type the group, Enter Shift+Enter
Anywhere New "Next group" and "Previous group" commands A "Swap to next group" command

A limit on "anywhere": Chrome lets an extension suggest default keys for only four commands, and Papaya already uses all four (open manager ⌘⇧K, copy link ⌘⇧1, screenshot ⌘⇧2, side panel ⌘⇧E). The new commands would ship without keys, and you'd pick them in chrome://extensions/shortcuts. The other option is to give up one of the four defaults.

7Smart grouping

One flow, three engines behind it. Every engine returns the same thing: a list of groups, each a name plus tab ids. The reply is checked against a schema and against your real tabs, so nothing is parsed out of free text. Suggestions are always reviewed before they're applied, and applying only creates tab groups.

8Your two questions

"Loading only the visible tab — we'd still be opening all 300 tabs, right?"

Yes, every tab would exist in the tab strip. The difference is that Chrome can keep a tab unloaded: it has its address, but no page runs until you click it. I measured it on hl-mango (Linux, Chrome 148) by opening the same 30 pages from Wikipedia, MDN, GitHub, Hacker News and vuejs.org both ways:

Opened normally
+5.1 GB
Created, then unloaded
+0.09 GB

Chrome's memory for 30 tabs: 1.2 → 6.2 GB opened normally, 1.2 → 1.3 GB unloaded. Page processes: 31 new vs none. The test box has no GPU, so absolute numbers run higher than on a Mac; the ratio is the point. Clicking an unloaded tab loaded it normally.

Is this something the Chrome API gives us?

Yes. chrome.tabs.discard(tabId) unloads a tab: it stays in the tab strip, and Chrome reloads it when you click it. It's the same mechanism as Chrome's Memory Saver. tab.discarded says whether a tab is unloaded, and tab.autoDiscardable controls whether Chrome may unload it on its own. It needs no extra permission.

There's no "create it unloaded" option, so Papaya creates each tab in the background and unloads it straight away. Each one starts its request for a moment; the measurement above includes that. The tab you're looking at can't be unloaded. In the test, all 30 tabs unloaded and kept their tab ids.

The catch: a tab unloaded before it ever loaded shows its address instead of its title, and no icon, until you click it:

Taxes Form 1040 – IRS irs.gov/forms… docs.google.c…

First tab clicked and loaded; the other two still unloaded.

In this model you'll rarely open hundreds of tabs anyway. Stashed groups stay in the sidebar, opening a stashed group opens just that group, and opening a session reopens only the groups that were open.

9Decisions

Decided

  • The top-level thing is called a Session; groups live inside sessions.
  • One session open at a time for now; several later if you miss it.
  • Auto-group groups in place, then you put groups away one by one. In v2, putting a group away is Stash.
  • History gets a new version when you close or switch away, plus a manual "Save version".
  • Unloaded tabs show their address until clicked; the group's name says what's inside.
  • Pinned tabs are global for now: never saved, never closed by Papaya. To revisit later.
  • Smart grouping escalates through three engines: by site always, the on-device model when the machine supports it, your agent once the hub exists.
  • Switching to a group focuses it (the other groups fold up); Shift swaps instead, once stashing exists.
  • The sessions library (phase 4) comes before stashing groups (phase 3).
  • A closed session's tabs can be edited: remove tabs or groups, rename groups. Edits save as a new version. Built.
  • Smart group moves strays into the window holding most of the group; Undo moves them back. Built.

Migration, once, at the switch-over

Steps 1–5 are built and tested; running them for real is phase 4.

  1. Rollback copy. The library is its own store, and the old store is left exactly as it was. Rolling back means using the old store again.
  2. What's open now becomes Home. If a context is active, that context becomes the open session instead. Chrome stays the source of truth for what's open.
  3. Each context becomes a session. Its current tabs and its snapshots become its versions, groups included. Archived stays archived; Parked keeps its role.
  4. Each saved session becomes a closed session with one version, named from its title or, when it has none, its sites. Copies the old save code wrote to the second store, exact duplicates and empty sessions are dropped; tabs pointing at a group that was never saved come back ungrouped.
  5. A dry run reports all of this first. Done on the July backup (results); it runs again on a fresh backup before the switch-over, and you confirm the result.
  6. Backups export in the new format; importing an old backup runs the same migration.
